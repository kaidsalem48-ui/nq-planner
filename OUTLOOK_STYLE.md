## Primary purpose: how the user interprets news

The user clarified that the screenshots were sent to teach how they read news
and how that changes their outlook. Prioritize this reasoning when preparing
future briefs. Formatting is secondary. Additional reference batches do not by
themselves request a new interface, a deployment or a new trading restriction.

Follow the chain: **news and current context → expectations and the main catalyst
→ plausible trading conditions → the user's saved plan**.

- Weigh releases by importance to the current backdrop; do not treat every event
  as equally significant. Identify what the period is building toward.
- Connect prior results, revisions and verified expectations to what the next
  release could confirm or challenge, particularly for inflation, employment,
  growth and the Fed outlook.
- Consider the period's sequence: a quiet early week ahead of a major release
  can call for patience; a crowded release/earnings day warrants extra selectivity.
  Describe these as possibilities, not guaranteed price paths.
- Interpret verified gaps, ranges, yields, dollar moves and earnings leadership
  alongside the calendar. Headline/geopolitical risk can matter even when the
  scheduled economic calendar is light.
- Translate that context into the user's familiar outlook language: potential
  chop or range compression, headline sensitivity, two-way volatility, gap-fill
  versus continuation scenarios, and where patience/selectivity matters.
- Apply the user's existing risk and timing rules. A reference outlook's opinion
  must not silently become a permanent rule, a numerical prediction or a change
  to trading/account settings. Verify current facts before using them.

Remember the analytical approach across monthly, weekly and daily outlooks;
do not memorize the screenshots' historical market claims as facts about today.

## Voice and structure

- Lead with the period and a short, readable overview of its main theme.
- Follow with **Key events**, listing the weekday/date, New York release time,
  and the actual event names. Distinguish high and medium impact.
- Explain why the main catalysts matter: inflation, employment, Fed policy,
  growth, or consumer demand. Highlight clusters of releases and quieter gaps.
- Add a brief **Trading focus** using the user's saved rules and conditions.
- Mention earnings, political events or geopolitical headlines only when their
  dates/context are verified. A quiet release calendar can still have headline risk.
- Write naturally, in short paragraphs and simple lists. Keep the text readable
  on a phone. Avoid unsupported bullish/bearish predictions and dramatic claims.

Do not reuse their historical dates, officeholders, earnings claims or macro

## Additional preferences from the second batch

The user wants an outlook that explains the setup for the period, beyond a release
list. Prefer the more detailed October/November examples when verified context
is available. Use the shorter March examples for a lighter period.

1. **Name the dominant catalyst.** Open with a clear theme such as jobs week,
   inflation week, Fed week, or a lighter calendar with headline risk. Explain
   what makes the main event important relative to the other scheduled releases.
2. **Connect recent data to the next event.** When sourced, mention the relevant
   previous result, revision, current consensus and policy backdrop. Clearly
   distinguish released facts, forecasts, market pricing and the writer's inference.
   Never carry a screenshot's payroll number, inflation print or rate level forward.
3. **Describe conditional scenarios.** Explain what a stronger/weaker or hotter/
   cooler release could change, when supported. Identify any sourced base case
   separately from alternatives. Use conditional language rather than promising
   consolidation, a breakout or a particular market response.
4. **Combine catalysts on the same day.** Include verified large-company earnings
   beside economic releases; label premarket versus after-close timing. Explain
   potential index relevance without inventing earnings dates or results.
5. **Add a global backdrop when it matters.** Dated reporting on energy, geopolitical
   tensions, rates, the dollar or global growth can explain why a sparse calendar
   still carries headline risk. Omit a stock geopolitical paragraph if no current
   evidence supports it.
6. **Make the timing practical.** Keep key-event bullets in weekday order, emphasize
   the main catalysts, and include exact New York release times. Holiday/open-market
   distinctions and Fed blackout periods need verification. An ET schedule alone
   does not establish another country's DST conversion.
7. **End with the user's actionable focus.** State the saved timing restrictions,
   selectivity and risk plan for the relevant days. Do not add a new 30-minute news
   blackout or a directional trading rule merely because a reference mentions it.

Preferred expanded structure: period heading; one short overview paragraph;
one optional sourced context/scenario paragraph; **Key events** by weekday;
optional **Global backdrop**; a short **Trading focus**. Use selective bold emphasis
for the major catalyst and times, and keep paragraphs readable on a phone.

Reference-specific patterns include a heavy multi-catalyst week spanning two
months, a lighter post-data week, bank-holiday thin-volume cautions, a Fed blackout
week, a Fed/GDP/PCE/earnings cluster, and a jobs week containing elections and a
US clock change. These are examples of how to organize context, not instructions
to import their calendar entries or claim their market narratives are true today.

## Additional preferences from the third batch

- Support a compact **What we're watching** / **Market watch** format: a few
  named themes with a short explanation of why each matters, followed by a
  clear volatility watch. Use this when a full event-by-event essay is unnecessary.
- Where verified, lead with a notable opening gap, previous-session close or
  range condition. Specify the instrument/contract, session and baseline:
  Sunday Globex versus Friday's close is different from today's RTH opening gap
  and from the defined 18:00-to-09:29 overnight net move. Do not invent a gap size
  or an all-time-high claim, or treat a gap as proof of the next direction.
- Describe gap fill versus continuation as competing scenarios to observe,
  rather than an expected outcome. The same applies to pre-release range
  tightening, chop and post-release expansion.
- Briefly connect inflation to Fed expectations, employment to the policy outlook,
  spending/housing to growth, and a verified earnings cluster to index leadership.
  Any actual figures, consensus values, trends or blackout dates need fresh sources.
- A holiday paragraph should distinguish the specific markets that are closed
  from those still open and frame low-volume risk without declaring a closure
  that the planner does not support.
- Cross-asset context (including crypto, energy, yields and the dollar) can be
  included when directly relevant to NQ and verified; avoid unrelated headlines.
- Selective bold labels and optional restrained icons may help scanning. Do not
  copy the source's all-bold paragraphs, promotional material or class schedules.

## Additional preferences from the fourth batch

- Compact weekly briefs can lead with three to five theme-led bullets rather
  than an introductory essay. Give each a short descriptive label and explain
  its relevance, not just its date: labor demand, business activity, producer
  inflation, consumer confidence, policy expectations or earnings leadership.
- Scale the outlook to the calendar. A lighter week may need only a few bullets
  and one sentence identifying the main remaining catalyst. Avoid padding it.
- When verified reporting supports a geopolitical section, explain the specific
  transmission channel (for example, energy supply, shipping, inflation or risk
  sentiment) rather than adding a generic tensions paragraph. Clearly distinguish
  observed cross-asset moves from possible future effects.
- Distinguish policy decisions, minutes, testimony, speeches and political policy
  headlines. They are different catalysts; verify the speaker, role, date and
  scheduled time rather than recycling a historical officeholder or event.
- Explain links between releases and policy expectations in conditional terms.
  A stronger payroll print does not mechanically guarantee rising yields, and a
  weak print does not automatically imply a cut. Interpret surprises and revisions
  against a verified consensus and the current policy backdrop when available.
- Earnings breadth or named-company examples should be verified and relevant to
  the selected period. Do not reuse a screenshot's company count or ticker list.
- Historical shutdowns, release delays and data-quality caveats may matter when
  current official sources confirm them; do not carry them into later periods.

## Scope

- **Monthly:** main themes, important release clusters, holidays/early closes,
  expiry dates and the distribution of lighter versus busier weeks.
- **Weekly:** concise overview, key events by weekday with times, and the main
  restrictions to remember for that week. Include cross-month weeks correctly.
- **Daily:** today's catalysts with times, saved day grade and rules, verified
  recorded market conditions with source/as-of time, and an easy path to schedule.

## Accuracy and automation

Calendar-based outlooks use the planner's current data and mark estimated dates.
Unknown coverage must not be described as an empty or safe calendar. Calendar
summaries are generated for the selected period; an 8:00 AM recorded snapshot
can add sourced market context without pretending to know later prices or data.
Market conditions are automatic and read-only. Legacy device observations do not override sourced recordings.

For richer daily commentary the recording automation may save `mcComment`, but
it must cite the actual same-session reporting in `sources` with `mcComment` in
the supported fields. Include a source/as-of time. Do not turn reported context
into a prediction. Dealer positioning and gamma must stay unknown without data.
